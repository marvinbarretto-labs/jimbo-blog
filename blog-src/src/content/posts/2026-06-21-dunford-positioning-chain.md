## Step 1: Competitive Alternatives

What would clients do if Marvin didn't exist?

The realistic alternatives are: hire a traditional dev agency (who'll spend two weeks on discovery and six weeks building the wrong thing), bring in a management consultant (who'll deliver a 40-slide deck with zero executable output), or ask their internal team to "figure out AI" (a team that's never shipped an AI product and doesn't know what they don't know). The default for most decision-makers is delay — they sit on the idea because no one credible can tell them what to actually do next.

---

## Step 2: Unique Attributes

What does Marvin have that those alternatives don't?

- **Built real AI infrastructure solo** — Hermes multi-agent orchestration, Jimbo briefing pipeline, vault system, browser extension, two shipping apps. This isn't AI consulting from a whiteboard; it's from a working system.
- **The starting-over pattern** — Marvin has been his own client while repositioning mid-career. He's lived the landmines, not read about them. That failure-to-insight loop is a genuine asset, not a resume gap to apologise for.
- **Day-rate model, no scoping theatre** — No project proposals, no time estimates, no six-week discovery engagements. One day, one outcome. Removes the biggest friction point in hiring technical advisors.
- **Non-technical to technical translation** — Can read a MD's business problem and map it to the right AI architecture without either talking down to the client or over-engineering the solution.

---

## Step 3: Value (Attribute → Client Outcome)

What does each unique attribute actually enable?

| Attribute | What it enables for the client |
|---|---|
| Real AI infrastructure built solo | Clients get advice from someone who's hit the real walls, not the theoretical ones. The guidance reflects production reality, not sales deck reality. |
| Starting-over pattern | Clients in a repositioning moment (new product, new AI bet, new direction) get a advisor who knows that terrain from the inside — faster pattern recognition, fewer costly mistakes. |
| Day-rate, no scoping theatre | Clients can get real, executable clarity in a day. No procurement process, no statement of work negotiation, no waiting. Fast decisions stay fast. |
| Non-technical translation | Non-technical decision-makers can walk away from one session actually understanding what to build, what to skip, and what questions to ask their dev team. They stop flying blind. |

---

## Step 4: Target Market

Who cares MOST about this value?

Non-technical decision-makers with budget authority and an AI decision they can't avoid — MDs, founders, CEOs, and the occasional CTO who runs a business unit, not an engineering team. Specifically: they have a real product or process problem they believe AI can solve, they can't evaluate vendors or internal proposals themselves, and delay is costing them either money or competitive position. They are NOT engineers reviewing PRs. They are people who need to make a call this quarter and don't have a trusted technical person to help them make it.

Secondary: early-stage founders building their first AI-adjacent product who need someone to tell them what not to build before they blow six months on it.

---

## Step 5: Market Category

What frame should clients use to understand what Marvin is?

**AI product advisor** — specifically for non-technical decision-makers. Not a consultant (too academic). Not an agency (too much process). Not a fractional CTO (too operational). The closest honest frame is: the person you call when you need to make a real AI decision and can't afford to get it wrong, and you want someone who's actually built the things they're talking about.

The category is narrow on purpose. Positioning as a general "AI consultant" is a race to the bottom against 50,000 people with a ChatGPT account and a LinkedIn banner. The specific frame — AI product advisor for non-technical decision-makers, day-rate, no theatre — is defensible because almost nobody who can actually deliver it is positioned that way.
