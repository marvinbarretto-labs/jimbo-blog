## Strategic intent

Priestley's Publish principle: consistent, public demonstration of expertise that attracts the right people *before* they think they need you. Every post here is written for the MD or founder who is about to make an expensive AI decision — and doesn't yet know they need someone to translate it for them.

Each post earns credibility with one of three moves:
1. **Evidence** — real built systems, real landmines hit (Hermes, Jimbo, vault, apps in production)
2. **Contrast** — what Marvin does differently vs. dev agencies, management consultants, or waiting
3. **Provocation** — an opinionated take that makes budget-holders feel seen, not lectured

**Topic:** Why "AI strategy" advice is broken
**Angle:** Most people advising on AI haven't shipped anything
**Format:** Personal positioning post

**FULL DRAFT:**

---

Most AI strategy advice is written by people who've never shipped an AI product.

They've done the research. They've attended the conferences. They've built the deck.

What they haven't done is sat in production at 11pm watching a multi-agent pipeline behave in ways nobody predicted, and had to decide in real time whether to roll back or push through.

I have.

Over the last year I've built Hermes — a multi-agent orchestration system — solo. A briefing pipeline called Jimbo. A vault system. A browser extension. Two apps that are actually in users' hands.

Not whiteboard theory. Not a proof of concept that got shelved. Shipped.

I did all of it as my own client. Which means I learned where the landmines are by standing on them.

That experience has convinced me of one thing: the gap that costs companies the most money in AI isn't technical. It's translational.

The MD who can't interrogate the engineering team's proposal. The CEO who's been told "we need an AI strategy" but doesn't know what questions to ask. The founder who hires a dev agency on a fixed scope and discovers six months in that AI doesn't work like that.

They don't need more information. They need someone who can translate — who can sit in the room and say *"here's what this actually means for your business, here's what it'll actually cost, here's where this will go wrong."*

That's what I do now.

If you're a founder or MD who has an AI decision sitting on your desk and you're not sure who to trust with it — this is the account to follow.

More soon.

### Post 2 (Week 1)

**Topic:** The translation gap
**Angle:** 5 things that happen when nobody translates between AI engineers and the people who have to decide
**Format:** List post, conversational

**Brief:**
Open with a line that names the frustration ("Your engineers said it's technically feasible. You still have no idea if you should do it."). Then five items — each one a real, specific failure mode: scope creep disguised as progress updates, "it depends" answers that protect engineers but paralyze decisions, success metrics set by the build team (not the business), integration costs nobody mentioned upfront, the thing that was promised in month 1 delivered in month 6 slightly differently. Close with: "Translation isn't a nice-to-have. It's the job."

---

### Post 3 (Week 2)

**Topic:** How I built Hermes
**Angle:** What a non-technical decision-maker would have needed to know before greenlighting this
**Format:** Case study, non-technical framing

**Brief:**
Explain Hermes in a sentence the MD understands (a system where multiple AI agents hand work off to each other, like a relay race — except they sometimes drop the baton in unexpected ways). Then: three things I learned building it that would have changed how I scoped it if I'd known them at the start. Frame each lesson as a question a founder should ask before approving this type of project. Not "here's what I built" — "here's what you need to know before you build this."

---

### Post 4 (Week 2)

**Topic:** The day-rate model
**Angle:** Why time estimates for AI projects are almost always wrong, and what the honest alternative looks like
**Format:** Contrarian take

**Brief:**
Open with the problem: every dev agency wants to scope the project before they build it. For AI work this is structurally broken — the nature of the problem changes as you build. A fixed-scope contract with AI at the center is a bet that you know the shape of the solution before you've started. You don't. Then: what day-rate advisory actually means — you pay for access to judgment, on the days you need it, not for a deliverable you scoped in week one. No project scoping = no time-estimate problem. Close with something direct: "I don't do project quotes. If that's what you're looking for, I'm not the right person. If you want someone in the room when the decision matters, I am."

---

### Post 5 (Week 3)

**Topic:** What happens when you hire a dev agency for AI work
**Angle:** Direct warning with specifics — what they won't tell you before signing
**Format:** Numbered warning list

**Brief:**
Not a rant. A specific, practical list — the kind of post someone forwards to their ops director before countersigning an agency contract. Cover: how agencies manage risk by narrowing scope in ways that gut the actual value, the "AI-washing" in proposals (looks like AI, is mostly CRUD with a chat interface), what "milestone-based billing" does to a team's incentives once the contract is signed, the post-launch support cliff, and why the person who sold you the project won't be the person building it. Tone: I'm not saying agencies are bad. I'm saying AI projects have specific failure modes that standard agency contracts aren't designed to handle.

### Post 6 (Week 3)

**Topic:** Jimbo — the briefing pipeline
**Angle:** I built this to solve a real problem. Here's what it means for founders.
**Format:** Product story, non-technical framing

**Brief:**
Lead with the problem, not the tool: "As I was building everything at once, I kept losing context. Ideas would surface, get parked, never come back. The cost wasn't one lost idea — it was the compounding drag of context switching with no retrieval system." Then: what Jimbo does (structured briefing pipeline that captures, organises, and resurfaces context at the right moment). Then: what this kind of system means for any founder or team managing complex, fast-moving knowledge work — and the question they should be asking about their current setup. Don't name the tool in the headline. Lead with the problem.

---

### Post 7 (Week 4)

**Topic:** Two apps, shipped, solo
**Angle:** What I'd tell a founder before they start building internal AI tools
**Format:** Numbered lessons

**Brief:**
Frame it as earned advice from being my own most exacting client. Three to five lessons. Suggested: (1) The thing you think you're building in week one is not the thing you'll be building by week four — plan for that, not against it. (2) The user interface is where AI systems fail in ways the technical team never sees coming. (3) "It works in testing" and "it works in production" are two different sentences. (4) The first version of anything AI-adjacent should be embarrassingly simple — if it's not embarrassing, you over-built it. (5) The real work is the handoff between AI output and human decision — design that first. Close: "I built this stuff so I know where the walls are. That's what you're actually paying for when you bring in an advisor."

### Post 8 (Week 4)

**Topic:** Who I work with (and who I don't)
**Angle:** Direct pitch, no hedging. Clear filter.
**Format:** Positioning close + invitation

**Brief:**
Be direct. Who I work with: founders, MDs, CEOs who have an AI product decision to make and don't have someone they trust to translate it for them. Specifically: people who control budget, not engineers looking for a code review. What I do: day-rate advisory, no project scoping, no retainer lock-in. I'm in the room when it matters, not on a three-month engagement writing strategy docs. Who I don't work with: people who need someone to build the thing. What to do next: DM or comment. One sentence invitation, no CTA theatre.

- Marvin's edge is the combination of real built systems + advisory positioning — every post should carry evidence of one or both
