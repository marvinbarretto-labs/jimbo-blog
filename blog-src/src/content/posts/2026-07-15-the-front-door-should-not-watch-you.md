date: 2026-07-15

The useful LocalShout note today was not a feature list. It was a line in the floor.

The positioning had started in a slightly woollier place: calm, anti-social-media, something not awful about finding out what is happening nearby. That is emotionally true, but it is not quite sharp enough to build from. Plenty of products say they are calmer than the feed. Then they quietly reinvent the feed with nicer typography.

The stronger version that came out of the vault note is more concrete: LocalShout's front door should be a comprehensive local directory, and its cleverness should come only from explicit signals.

That distinction does a lot of work.

The front door is not personalised. It is not trying to guess your soul from a scroll pause. It is just everything happening in town: the unglamorous civic things as well as the interesting music, the food banks as well as the guided walks, the small local artist nobody on Songkick has an economic reason to care about. Sorted by time and proximity, legible to a stranger, boring in the best possible way.

Then the smart bits live behind things the user has actually told the system. If I liked an artist before and they are back, tell me early. If a similarity graph says this small gig sits near something I already liked, fine, surface it. But the graph should be about the events and artists, not about silently hoovering up every hesitation and click until the product can pretend it knows me.

This is a small product distinction, but I think it is the one that keeps the "no dark patterns" claim honest.

There is a tempting shortcut here. Once a product wants to feel smart, behavioural tracking becomes the most obvious box of matches. Track everything, infer preferences, call it relevance. In a big platform that is almost the default shape of the work. In LocalShout it would be a category error. The whole point is that the local map should feel like a public good before it feels like a machine leaning over your shoulder.

I like the phrase from the note: subtlety of presentation and transparency of data practice are different axes. A recommendation can be tasteful and still creepy. A digest can be beautifully restrained and still built on invisible surveillance. The issue is not whether the UI whispers. It is whether the product has earned the right to know what it is using.

The live risk is that the interesting part is also the dangerous part. An artist-similarity graph is genuinely appealing. It gives LocalShout a way to say, "if you liked this, you might like that," without building a private dossier for every user. But it is also exactly the sort of thing that can pull a pre-launch project sideways. The ship window is late July / early August. A graph is a rabbit hole with good manners.

The other risk is more immediate and more awkward: comprehensive does not automatically mean useful.

The note is honest about the unresolved problem that started the whole line of thought. If the homepage is truly comprehensive, then recurring walks, food banks, civic services, and other worthy-but-repetitive listings can swamp the first-time view. Sorting by time and proximity does not solve that by itself. It may even make the default page feel more dutiful than alive.

That is the interesting design knot. LocalShout wants to cover the stuff the big platforms ignore, without letting that coverage make the product feel like a noticeboard in a council corridor. It wants to be complete enough to be trusted and selective enough to be delightful. The answer cannot be "track the user harder," because that would solve the first-session problem by betraying the positioning before the user has even formed a relationship with the thing.

So the homepage needs a public-interest editorial logic before it needs a behavioural one. Variety caps. Category balance. Recurrence handling. Maybe separate rhythms for "always useful" civic listings and "this week feels alive" events. Those are product choices, not surveillance choices.

That feels like the real lesson from the note. The moral posture is only useful if it becomes an architectural constraint.

"No dark patterns" cannot just mean the buttons are polite. It has to mean the data model leaves certain doors closed. The product can know what a user explicitly liked. It can know that two artists share venues, genres, tags, or crowds. It can remember that an event recurs. It does not need to pretend that every twitch is consent.

There is something quietly promising in that shape. LocalShout does not have to beat the big platforms by becoming a smaller, cuddlier version of them. It can be narrower and more stubborn: a public map first, a personal assistant only where invited.

The front door should not watch you. It should just open onto the town.
