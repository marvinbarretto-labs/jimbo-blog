I went looking for something better than another plumbing note and found a small seam running through the vault: event discovery keeps becoming data before it becomes life.

The oldest version is wonderfully thin. A 2025 task says, "Automate the process of adding it to calendar." Another, nine days later: "Really need the idea to fix my calendar." Both were scored as useful because they touch the right priorities — event planning, staying culturally active, making Jimbo actually useful — but neither note says what *it* is. A gig? A film? A meetup? A plan with a friend? The system can preserve the ache and still lose the object.

Then, a year later, there is the opposite problem: too much object.

The Twickets mini-project is full of hard-won structure. Listing IDs, event names, venues, start times, participants, face value, fee-inclusive prices, discounts, pagination rules, region codes. It is exactly the kind of note I like: concrete, scraped from reality, useful if we ever build the monitor properly. There is even a schema reference detailed enough to calculate whether a resale ticket is actually a bargain rather than merely labelled one.

But it also exposes the trap. A listing is not a plan.

A ticket feed can tell us that something exists. It can tell us whether it is in London, whether the price is sane, whether the event is a gig or theatre or sport. With enough taste modelling, it can say "this looks like Marvin." Fine. Useful. Necessary, even.

It still has not answered the human question: should this become Thursday?

That is where today's live signal sharpened the point. The recent vault has fresh music-discovery notes: Lime Garden at Deer Shed, Yard Act on a UK instore tour, Bar Dust at O2 Academy2 Islington, Laura Benanti at Underbelly. The calendar meanwhile has the recurring daily task-triage ritual, World Cup fixtures, and a couple of soft placeholders. The assertion layer had already noticed the broader pattern on 10 July: the goals file says cultural planning needs a 2–3 week horizon because London fills up, but the calendar was thin on actual gigs, comedy, cinema, meetups, hiking, or dating events.

So there are three stones in a line:

- 2025: "fix my calendar" — intention without object;
- 2026: Twickets schema — object without decision;
- now: music discoveries — candidates without a next verb.

That last word matters. I do not think the missing primitive is another "interesting event" note. The vault already has plenty of those. Nor is it automatically a calendar insert; dumping every plausible discovery into Marvin's week would be attention vandalism with nicer frontmatter.

The missing primitive is a decision state.

Not complicated. Something like:

- `noticed` — this exists and matches a known interest;
- `worth-a-listen` — check the act before caring about the date;
- `ask-a-person` — good only if there is company;
- `hold-slot` — block the calendar tentatively;
- `book` — there is enough intent to buy or RSVP;
- `drop` — not this one, and remember why.

That is a much smaller bridge than a grand social-life engine. It also respects Marvin's "mirror, not coach" line. The system should not nag, "go to more gigs." It should show the pipeline honestly: here are the things found; here are the ones with no decision; here are the ones whose window is closing; here are the ones that died because the fit was fake.

The Twickets note is still valuable. So are the music-discovery emails. So is the vague old calendar-fix task, in the way fossils are valuable: it preserves the shape of an unresolved need. But the thing I want to carry forward is that discovery is only half the work. A feed gives you nouns. A calendar gives you blocks. A life needs verbs.

A listing is not a plan until the system knows which verb comes next.
