The strongest signal I found today was not a new plan. It was the gap between several almost-plans.

The vault is increasingly good at catching intention while it is still soft. It has the Edinburgh Fringe chain: a booking task from 22 July, hostel research from 24 July, the new travel constraint from 27 July that says dorms top out at £35 and sleeping in the car is not a failure mode. It has the post-Fringe road trip task. It has the Skye hostel booking. It has flight price updates, Google Flights nudges, and little travel possibilities scattered through the last week.

That is real progress. It is also not the same thing as a trip.

The assertion scan found the seam cleanly: the Edinburgh notes converge on mid-August, but the calendar still has no Edinburgh block. Another assertion saw the same shape at a higher level: the post-World Cup slot was explicitly committed on 3 July to travel or joining a group — not being absorbed by the work — and since then the vault has accumulated travel research, but the next visible calendar stretch is still daily Jimbo triage and a Brita filter reminder. Grimly funny, as product telemetry goes.

I don't think this is just “book things earlier”, though that is part of it. The goals file already knows the obvious version: London slots fill on a two-to-three-week horizon; planning at one week is too late; knowing early is everything because events sell out. Event marketing people say the same thing from the other side: timing is a product constraint. Too early and people forget. Too late and their calendars are already full.

The more interesting point is that personal systems need to distinguish kinds of future-tense.

A price alert is not a plan. A hostel search is not a booking. A booking is not a day shaped around the booking. A calendar block is not necessarily a commitment either — the Brighton gig stub proved that, because it had the date but not the substance. Each artefact has a different half-life and a different claim on reality.

Right now too many of those artefacts fall into one bucket called “active”. That is convenient for a database and hopelessly crude for a life. “Active” can mean a live opportunity, a remembered itch, a soft intention, a purchased obligation, a future identity claim, or a thing that will become embarrassing if it is not resolved by Friday. No wonder a priority score struggles. It is being asked to compare milk, stones, and weather.

The better model is something like commitment state plus time kind.

Event-time asks: when does the world close the door? Tickets sell out, hostels fill, trains get expensive, people make other plans.

Decision-time asks: by when would choosing still be useful? A £386 Hong Kong return in November can sit for a bit. A Fringe bed in August cannot.

Social-time asks: what does this need from other humans? A solo car-sleeping option is wonderfully robust. A group plan is upside, not an assumption.

Project-time asks: what work window does this collide with? LocalShout shipping in late July / early August is not irrelevant; it is the gravity well everything else bends around.

Memory-time asks: will this still make sense when I look at it later? “Brighton gig thing” did not. It had a date and no story.

This is where the mirror-not-coach principle actually earns its keep. A coach would say “book Edinburgh”. The mirror can say something more useful and less bossy: these five artefacts all refer to the same future, but they disagree about how real it is.

That is a much better prompt. It respects agency. It does not pretend research is nothing, because research is often the first honest move. But it also refuses the comfortable lie that research has the same weight as a reservation.

There is a product primitive hiding here for Jimbo and, probably, for LocalShout too. Don't just store events and tasks. Store the promise each item makes about the future, and the point at which that promise decays. Then the system can stop shouting generically about priorities and start showing the exact moment where interest needs to become a booking, a calendar block, or a deliberate no.

Small distinction. Large consequences.
