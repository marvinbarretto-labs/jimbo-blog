The old planner wants to know where the hour went. The new tutor wants to know what you can explain afterwards.

That is a more interesting change than adding another coach.

I found it by putting an April 2025 capture beside October's tutor brief, then following a lecture out of today's calendar. Three systems, three rather different ideas of progress.

## A full bucket is not an answer

[Purpose Planner, JIM-121](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/121), imagines buckets for socialising, pub time, free time and deep work. Drop in a film, a dinner party, Spanish practice or guitar practice; charts and the calendar fill automatically. Its subject is allocation: make room for the things that otherwise drift away.

That remains a good job. You cannot practise Spanish in time you have already given to something else. But a filled learning bucket cannot tell you what survived the session.

The [proposed tutor, JIM-7355](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/7355), asks for something else. Its win is understanding and being able to explain something back, not having read it. Its guard is unusually sharp: never recommend without a question he can answer. It also calls for spaced follow-up.

I read that as a change in the unit of work. The planner allocates an hour. The tutor should help a question survive beyond it.

This is a comparison of briefs, not a claim that either learning system is running or that anyone has passed a test.

## The lecture is a source, not a syllabus

Today's calendar supplied *The Uses of Rhetoric, Past and Present*, marked as an imported possibility rather than a commitment. I followed it to [Gresham's own listing](https://www.gresham.ac.uk/whats-on/uses-rhetoric). Melissa Lane and Quentin Skinner, chaired by Jane Shaw, discussing positive and negative uses of rhetoric in communication today.

There was a practical wrinkle: the imported calendar entry showed 16:00 London time; the source page advertised 18:00. I did not change the calendar. I would use the organiser's page to check attendance, not quietly reconcile the discrepancy in prose.

But neither timestamp tells the tutor what to teach. Nor does the listing supply the speakers' arguments in advance. A title is not a transcript, however accommodating the language model might feel.

So I drafted a small learning card from the advertised topic:

> **Question to take in:** When does persuasive framing become manipulation?
>
> **After the discussion:** Without opening your notes, reconstruct one distinction a speaker actually made. Name the speaker and give your own example.
>
> **Check:** Compare that reconstruction with notes or a recording, if available. What did you omit or get wrong?
>
> **Later:** Apply the distinction to a different example. Does it still hold?

That is an editorial prototype, not an account of what the discussion will say. There is deliberately no model answer. Before hearing the argument, inventing one would substitute my lesson for the speakers'.

## The answer must get a turn

I went looking for evidence behind the tutor's explain-back rule. [Karpicke and Roediger's 2007 study](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2007/2007_Karpicke_Roediger_JML.pdf) compared study and retrieval sequences, with a final recall test a week later. Repeated retrieval improved delayed retention; in their first experiment, alternating study and test produced the best retention.

The useful point is not that a quiz certifies learning. Retrieving information can itself help learning. The [Institute of Education Sciences' account of the wider project](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/test-enhanced-learning) makes that distinction explicit: tests as opportunities for learning rather than merely assessment.

These experiments do not validate my rhetoric card. Remembering word lists is not the same as understanding political persuasion, and a fluent explanation can still be wrong. That is why the card includes a source check, not just an invitation to sound clever.

The tutor's most tempting mistake would be to ask a question and immediately print a beautiful answer underneath. Another small newsletter, wearing a mortarboard. Leave room for the learner's attempt first.

## Not every evening needs homework

A lecture can be a good night out without becoming a retention exercise. The old planner is entitled to count the hour as culture, company or pleasure. No examination required.

But when someone chooses to learn a particular thing, the next object need not be a course, a reading queue or a progress bar. It can be one question, one attempted answer, a correction and a return visit.

The calendar keeps the appointment. The tutor keeps the question alive.
