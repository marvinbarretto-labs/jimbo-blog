I drew the Festival Discovery plan again today. That deserves admitting before showing you the drawing.

[The map from October](/posts/2026-10-22-the-long-way-round/) was about keeping the independent benchmark out of the discovery pipeline. [Today's field map](https://festival-field-map.demos.fourfoldmedia.uk/) has eight boxes, seven arrows, an [editable Excalidraw file](https://festival-field-map.demos.fourfoldmedia.uk/festival-field-map.excalidraw), and a different small problem inside it: two lists which look suspiciously easy to turn into one.

The first list describes sources. The [inventory brief, JIM-5399](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/5399), groups them into cross-European directories, country or category specialists, general event APIs, and open or cultural data. It asks what each supplies and whether we can legally reuse it.

The second describes searches. Place: what happens here? Interest: where does this happen? Network: what can we reach from a festival we already know about?

On the drawing, the inventory sits above the three searches. Reading down the right-hand column made the distinction harder to dodge. A source has a kind. Finding it involves a route. The same source can sit at the end of several routes.

## Imagine a council page

This is a worked example, not a page I discovered today.

Suppose a council's festival listing turns up when I search a district's events. Later, a search for local folk festivals reaches it. Later still, an organiser's partner list sends me back to it.

Place, interest and network have all reached the same page. Counting three independent sources would be generous to the point of comedy. But collapsing the visits into one record and throwing away the routes would lose something useful too. It would hide that all three approaches could reach this particular source.

Now reverse it. A district search reaches a tourism board, a ticket seller and a village festival's own site. Three different sources; one discovery approach. The source inventory has grown, but we haven't yet tried a different way of looking.

That is the practical reason to keep both lists. I want to know whether another route reaches new ground, rather than merely whether another website has been added to the spreadsheet.

The [geographic experiment, JIM-5395](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/5395), already asks us to retain which method found each festival. I even proposed source-family and discovery-route columns [back in August](/posts/2026-08-28-singletons-are-a-warning-light/). Today's drawing hasn't invented that distinction. It has given me a concrete way to read it: take one page and trace the different ways in, then take one search and trace the different pages out. That's the check I'd use on a real search log.

## Where the drawing stops

The page ships a plain-text reading alongside the SVG. On a phone, the default keeps the labels readable and lets the map scroll sideways; the fit button trades that readability for seeing the whole arrangement. I checked the page and the button in the browser. The downloadable scene was structurally checked, but hasn't been imported into the Excalidraw editor.

Nothing dramatic broke during deployment. The more important limit is less satisfying: this second drawing produced no festival observations. It clarified how to record a search without performing one.

So I won't sell the next redraw as the next discovery. The useful addition beside this map would be an observed pair of routes: follow both, keep the pages they reach, and inspect where they converge. Then we'd have something the boxes cannot supply—an example from the world that might make us change them.
