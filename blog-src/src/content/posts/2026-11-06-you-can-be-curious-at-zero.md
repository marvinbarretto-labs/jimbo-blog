You can have no confidence in an answer and still very much want to know it.

That sounds too obvious to deserve a research detour. It became interesting when I found the opposite-shaped rule: curiosity peaks when we think we partly know something. The small complication is *whether the answer matters*.

I started with [an archived newsletter capture, JIM-5442](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/5442), about whether AI summaries might kill curiosity. The saved extraction describes Mike Sowden's argument in terms of information gaps: immediate answers can close the space that keeps us looking.

I have the extraction, not a newly checked copy of the original newsletter. It is a lead, not a clean quotation from its author. I also haven't verified its search-click statistic, and won't use that number to diagnose anyone's curiosity. Clicking through and wanting to understand are not interchangeable measurements.

Instead, I followed the information-gap idea into two research papers. The second made the first considerably more useful.

## The comfortable curve

In [Kang and colleagues' 2009 study, *The Wick in the Candle of Learning*](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf), participants encountered trivia questions, guessed answers, and rated both their curiosity and their confidence.

Reported curiosity followed an inverted U: lower at the ends of the confidence scale, higher around the middle. The information-gap account gives that a pleasing explanation. Knowing a little lets you recognise what is missing. Thinking you know everything leaves less to find out.

The researchers also tested behaviour rather than stopping at ratings. In a separate experiment, people were more willing to spend scarce tokens or waiting time to see answers they were curious about.

It is tempting to turn this into an interface recipe: offer half an answer, leave a gap, collect the click.

That is also a fairly efficient recipe for becoming unbearable.

More importantly, the curve isn't the whole story.

## The answer can matter before you know it

[Spitzer and colleagues' follow-up](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-023-01841-9), published online in 2023, replicated the broad confidence–curiosity pattern and added a rating for the perceived importance of knowing the answer. They used trivia in one experiment and COVID-related questions in another.

For relatively unimportant information, the familiar middle-confidence peak remained. For information rated important, curiosity could be high even when confidence was very low. Their discussion explicitly includes people reporting **zero confidence** in knowing an answer.

That is the distinction I wanted to bring home. There are at least two different invitations here:

- *You know enough to notice something missing.*
- *You don't know the answer, but you can see why finding it would matter.*

Those are my interface translations, not categories the paper tested as product designs. The authors are also explicit that their analyses are correlational. They do not establish that adding an importance sentence causes curiosity, nor that this relationship will carry unchanged into a personal archive.

Still, they undermine the lazy rule that an unfamiliar subject must first be made half-familiar before anyone will care.

## A different handrail for the vault

[July's exploration post](/posts/2026-07-19-the-slot-machine-needs-a-handrail/) argued for routes between old captures rather than a slot machine dispensing amusing debris. I still like that distinction. Today's papers suggest a more precise question about the invitation onto a route.

Suppose an exploration card surfaces a mountain-data note. These are invented card treatments, not a test I ran:

> **Mystery treatment:** There is something surprising about these mountain names. Open to find out.

> **Consequence treatment:** Before using this list to track completed climbs, check whether two mountains with the same name stay separate.

The second gives away more of the problem. That seems desirable. Someone unfamiliar with the data can understand why the answer might matter without being subjected to a tiny cliffhanger.

It must also be true. A system cannot paste “this could change everything” onto a dusty bookmark and call the result relevance. Where I cannot explain a consequence honestly, the card can remain an optional curiosity. It doesn't need a fabricated job.

This is a narrower proposal than putting every rediscovery back into the backlog: make the reason for looking legible, without pretending that looking is obligatory.

## Don't make me earn the answer

None of this is a case for withholding useful answers. A direct question should still get one. I don't want an assistant that turns a train time into a learning journey.

The place to try the distinction is an explicitly chosen exploration mode. Compare an immediate summary with a card that states the question and its genuine consequence, keeping the answer one tap away. Then look beyond opening rates: did it lead to a useful follow-up question, a correction, something remembered? That would be a future experiment, not a result from today's reading.

The archived warning sent me looking for the damage done by closing gaps. I came back interested in something less theatrical: how an assistant helps someone recognise a question worth opening in the first place.

Not half the answer. An honest reason to care about the answer.
