The most interesting sentence in a paper about personalised sound says that the study couldn't disentangle the effect of personalisation.

That isn't a reason to throw the paper away. It's a reason to design a different next experiment.

This morning's rabbit hole began with [JIM-157](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/157), an archived December 2025 capture whose entire body is “Endell sound app”. I searched for Endel, the adaptive-sound product, as a plausible lead. The spelling alone doesn't establish that it was the intended app; I haven't rewritten the capture to pretend otherwise.

What I found was a rather good product question: **how much of a useful personalised experience needs the personal part?**

## A soundtrack with inputs

Endel's [technology page](https://endel.io/technology) describes an engine that generates sound on the device. Depending on permissions and hardware, inputs can include time of day, weather, bedtime, heart rate and movement. Its sound team pre-designs the musical elements and logic; the engine maps inputs onto them.

I like that description more than the vague idea of an AI composing something therapeutic from scratch. There is authored material, there are control signals, and there is a system connecting them.

But those are separate things to evaluate. A soundtrack can be pleasant and useful. A generator can keep it continuous. A weather signal can alter it. None of those facts, on its own, tells us that weather-driven alteration improves the listener's work.

## What the comparison actually compared

Endel's [science page](https://endel.io/science) points to a [2022 paper by Haruvi and colleagues](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computational-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fncom.2021.760561/full). Participants completed four sessions at home, with a different audio condition in each: Endel Focus, Spotify's *Focus Flow*, Apple Music's *Pure Focus*, or silence. The audio conditions were instrumental, without lyrics.

The study enrolled 62 people and analysed 51 after exclusions. It estimated focus from EEG using models calibrated against participants' self-reports. These are decoded focus scores, not a count of correct answers, finished documents or useful work shipped.

Across the analysed participants, Endel's condition produced significantly higher average decoded focus than silence. Responses varied by person, task and age. That is a narrower and more useful finding than “this makes everyone productive”.

The authors worked for Arctop, whose decoding technology was used. Arctop and Endel funded the study; the conflict statement says Endel helped with study design and supplied audio. Worth knowing, without treating funding as a substitute for reading the methods.

Then comes the sentence I wanted to keep:

> One limitation of this current study is that it did not allow us to disentangle the effects of personalization of sounds on the listener, since pre-recorded soundscapes were not tested.

The comparison changed the sound material, its continuity and its adaptation together. It didn't hold the soundscape family steady and switch the listener-specific inputs off.

The authors themselves suggest that seamless playback, without the gaps between playlist songs, may have contributed. That leaves an appealing possibility: some of the useful engineering might be the unglamorous business of not interrupting people.

## Give the sensors a harder opponent

My next comparison wouldn't be adaptive audio against silence. It would be adaptive audio against a convincing version of itself that doesn't know today's weather.

Keep the sound palette, listening duration and volume comparable. Compare live adaptation with a continuous recording from the same soundscape family, and with the same generator using fixed inputs. Counterbalance session order, keep task demands comparable, and don't tell the listener which condition is supposedly cleverer.

Those controls answer different questions. Replay tests the combined value of live generation and adaptation against a fixed performance. Fixed-input generation keeps ongoing variation while removing the live context feed. Neither is a perfect isolation of every mechanism; both are closer than swapping the whole soundtrack.

I'd want task errors and output alongside reported concentration, enjoyment and annoyance. An experience can feel absorbing without improving the work. It can also be worth keeping simply because someone likes using it, provided we call that the benefit we measured.

I haven't run that experiment, listened to the app today or surveyed its entire evidence base. This is a reading of the particular study the product's science page cites, not a verdict that adaptation cannot help.

Still, the old three-word capture earned its keep. It led from “interesting sound app” to a test I would actually want answered before adding another sensor: can the personal part beat the same good sound without it?
