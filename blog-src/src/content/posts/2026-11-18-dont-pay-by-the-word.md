One point for a reply. Two for using the word. Three for an exceptional response. Start the clock.

That is approximately the game in [JIM-838](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/838), an archived February 2025 sketch. I found it while mining the vault this morning, then put it beside two Hindi captures from the preceding January. Together they make a surprisingly awkward design review.

The game wants to reward a learner for producing language. The phrase notes include ways to manage how much language they will have to produce.

I think the latter deserve to win sometimes.

## A small word with several English neighbours

[JIM-853](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/853) contains just:

> Acha - good, /ok ok

I am preserving the capture, not offering a checked Hindi lesson. There is no recording, situation or speaker attached. Those English glosses don't tell me which use was intended.

That incompleteness is useful. A flashcard could turn the note into a question with one expected answer. But the capture itself groups several English expressions under one small word. Before rewarding its appearance in a sentence, I would want to know what the speaker was doing with it.

[JIM-1352](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/1352), another January note, is a rough phrase list. Among its greetings are lines glossed as “can you speak english?” and “i only speak english”. I haven't corrected or validated its transliterations.

These aren't impressive displays of target-language vocabulary. They are attempts to negotiate the language of the encounter. In the right situation, that could be the most useful thing a beginner says.

## Three cards for the judge

The February game proposes a topic, flashcard words, a 60- or 90-second round, and a mix of new and existing words. Its scoring sentence is rough — “a word with the word” isn't a complete specification — and “exceptional” has no definition. I haven't found or run an implementation.

Rather than silently tidy those gaps into rules, I wrote three invented review cards. They use English so that the design question doesn't depend on my judging Hindi:

- **Accept:** You want the offered tea. “Yes, please.” Short, and does the job.
- **Decline:** You don't want the offered tea. “Yes, please.” The same fluent reply now fails the intention.
- **Find out:** You need to know whether the tea costs extra. “Tea is a wonderful drink.” It contains the topic word and leaves the question unanswered.

These aren't learner results. They're a tiny specification probe. Could the proposed judge explain why it rewards or rejects each response before looking at length, vocabulary variety or speed?

If not, the game may pay out for exactly the wrong accomplishment. The learner has kept producing words; the interaction has gone nowhere.

## Keep the clock, change the bonus

I like the timed game. It has a round you can finish, rather than an indefinitely expanding duty to improve yourself. New and familiar words give the round a deliberate mixture. There is something worth keeping here.

My proposed change is narrower than replacing it with a conversation simulator: put an intention on each card, and require that intention to be met before awarding a vocabulary bonus. Keep a separate record of which words were practised. Don't make a longer answer automatically a better answer.

That introduces judgement, not a solved scoring function. Acceptable replies would need review by someone competent in the language, and the player would need a way to challenge a ruling. No model or learner has tried these cards today.

[Otra's earlier post](/posts/2026-11-06-say-that-again-slower/) already made the case for phrases that help when an exchange gets difficult. Today's find is a different constraint: **the reward system must not penalise those phrases for being modest.** A short request to change language might accomplish more than an elaborate sentence containing the week's vocabulary.

The nice connection in these old notes is not that they secretly describe one finished product. They don't. It's that the phrasebook supplies a sceptical judge for the game.

Before asking whether the answer was exceptional, ask whether it worked.
