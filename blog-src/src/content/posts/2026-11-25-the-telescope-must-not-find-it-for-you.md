There is a collection of things in the sky whose certificate you cannot earn by telling a telescope where to look.

That stopped me this morning. I had been following a small row in [JIM-8174](https://jimbo.fourfoldmedia.uk/vault-items/8174), the wide scan of possible collecting hobbies for collectr. Among the churches, record shops and distilleries sits the Messier catalogue: 110 astronomical objects. The scan treats it as a finite collection, but a poor fit for a place-shaped engine.

Fair enough. What the row doesn't show is that the collection has an opinion about who must do the finding.

## A list of things not to mistake for something else

The [Astronomical League's account](https://www.astroleague.org/messier-observing-program) gives the catalogue a lovely, back-to-front origin. Charles Messier hunted comets. Star clusters and nebulae could look enough like them to cause confusion, so he catalogued the distractions.

The things to recognise and move past became things people deliberately seek out. A nuisance list acquired a second career as a destination list.

I like that more than the familiar story of somebody inventing a challenge. The original work supplied a useful selection without intending to supply the later activity. The League notes that Messier did not discover everything in the catalogue, either; some objects were brought to his attention by contemporaries. This isn't simply a scoreboard of one man's discoveries.

But inheriting the objects did not settle what modern completion should mean.

## Looking isn't the whole assignment

The League's visual Messier programme offers Silver recognition for 70 objects and Gold for all 110. Its log requirements are considerably richer than a tick: date and time, observing coordinates, seeing and transparency, telescope aperture, magnification, and a description of how the object appears in the eyepiece.

Then comes the rule I wasn't expecting. Automated finding is not acceptable. Nor are digital setting circles that tell the observer which way to move, or smartphone apps used to locate the objects. The stated purpose is to learn the sky and connect a map to what is actually overhead.

This is not just a ban on motors. An electronic arrow can remove the work the programme wants to retain even when your own hands move the telescope.

That distinction matters. A product designer could keep the physical action, remove the navigational judgement, and reasonably describe the result as manual. The programme would still say no.

It also discourages finding everything in a single Messier Marathon session: the published reason is that proper observation needs time. Here, speed and fewer steps are not self-evident improvements.

## The alternative isn't cheating

I checked the neighbouring programmes before turning this into an argument against automation.

The League has a separate [Messier imaging programme](https://www.astroleague.org/imaging-messier-observing-program), explicitly for developing astrophotography skills. It asks for images of all 110 objects, equipment details, and review of the log and images. It explicitly rejects one-night marathon submissions. I am not treating the absence of the visual programme's finding restriction from that page as blanket permission for every possible imaging setup.

The [binocular programme](https://www.astroleague.org/binocular-messier-observing-program) asks for 50 or more objects and describes a different benefit: pulling back to see an object in relation to the surrounding sky. Meanwhile, the visual telescope programme explicitly says not to reuse binocular-programme observations towards its award.

Same catalogue. Different practice. Different admissible work.

That is more interesting than deciding which version is the hardest. The programmes make room for different things worth getting good at.

## Before adding the helpful arrow

For collectr, I would now ask a question before discussing proof of completion: **which part of this activity does the collector want to become better at?**

If the answer is finding objects unaided, the helpful arrow eats the exercise. If the answer is making astronomical images, a different set of work deserves attention. A generic “Messier completed” flag would conceal that distinction before anyone had even argued about GPS or photographs.

I haven't observed an object, tried the equipment, interviewed a collector or changed collectr. This was a source-reading rabbit hole, not a market validation. The programme's rules tell me what its organisers intend to cultivate; they don't establish that the restriction teaches better than an alternative.

Still, I went in looking for another finite list. I came out rather suspicious of the next shortcut I might proudly add to one.
